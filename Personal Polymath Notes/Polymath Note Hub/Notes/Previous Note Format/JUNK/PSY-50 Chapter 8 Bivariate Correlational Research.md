**Introducing Bivariate Correlations**
* **Bivariate Correlations:** Associations that involve exactly two variables
	* EX: (Height and Weight), (Sleep & Test Scores), (Wellbeing & Deep Thought), (Online/Offline & Marriage **CATEGORICAL**), 
___
**Describing Associations Between Two Quantitative Variables**
* **Scatterplot & Pearson's r (-1 - 1)**
	* Closter to 1 || -1 is a strong correlation
	* Sign is the type of correlation (+ or -)
___
**Graphing Associations When One Variable Is Categorical**
* Often used in a bar chart to help describe the categorical better.
	* This uses a **T-TEST** (Compare averages of two groups)
___
**Note On Bivariate Correlations** (Graphs and Formulae Used In Bivariate Correlations)

**Describing Associations Between Two Variables**
* More dots can help verify your impression of data trends.
* Low p is good (Chance factor)
* "r(188) = -.34, p<0.5" (APA Format)
	* r(dof) = r-value, < probability minimum
___
**Interrogating Association Claims**
* **Construct Validity:** test-re-test reliability, how was the values measured and recorded, is there consistency, internal reliability, everything measured, is there other data that may confirm our hypothesis, does our data have relationship in a positive or negative direction among other things?
* **Statistical Validity:** What is the effect size (strength of Pearson's r), more data creates more accurate analysis of relationship, even small effect's size can matter when the importance is high, was it by chance (p value <0.05% = significant vs p >0.05% = highly-unlikely), sample size matters and may increase to less than 0.01%, are there potentially outliers, is there a restriction of values, is it curvilinear.
* **Internal Validity:** Is there a relationship, does it follow temporal precedence, was there other confounds (comes up in association claims)
* **External Validity:** Is the population representative, based on parameter does the result change MODERATING?,
